Mea Maxima Culpa

Posted on July 26, 2004 by Ken Parish
Posted in Print media

Mea culpa. Mea maxima culpa. Apparently the ABC has finally been cowed and beaten by the Right Wing Death Beasts. And I have to confess I've been a (small) part of the problem by occasionally joining the chorus of criticism of Auntie's evident left-wing bias. Of course, if they'd made a greater effort to balance the ubiquitous LWDBs with a few righties, Auntie might never have attracted the opprobrium of the right wing bovver boys in the first place. But still ...

Commissioning market research to measure the extent of bias in current affairs coverage was the first signal that the ABC had run up the white flag on political independence. But David Marr's Media Watch tonight provided the definitive indication that Auntie has surrendered. As Marr commented, refusing to provide controversial film clips to "advocacy or cause"-based docos (whatever that means) is blatant political censorship:

The last person who should determine the "editorial integrity" of embarrassing interview grabs and public statements is the embarrassed politician.

The ABC's gutless policy is an invitation to political censorship by the politicians themselves.

It's a disgrace.
Dead right. This is scarey stuff. I'm sorry David. You're a prissy, condescending wanker (not to mention hopelessly biased), but indispensible* just the same.

* I can't help wondering whether Marr would have covered the story if the doco-maker had been a RWDB sponsored by Riotinto or the Salvation Army instead of a leftie backed by Oxfam and Amnesty International. But it's a noble cause just the same.

When we talk about "the ABC", the immediate debate is about "ABC News and Current Affairs", which is independent of the Board in a way that the rest of the ABC isn't, as a consequence of Act of Parliament which has annoyed the Board ever since the late '40s.

    It is a pity that the discussion of national broadcasters is couched in such ideological terms because it replaces a more constructive debate. As has happened in so many institutions, the question of doing it better is replaced by organisations fighting for survival, being abolished or flogged off. Even in the US, the PBS network of public community stations performs this function (although not as well as the ABC). Here's a summary of its sources of revenue from the PBS website:

    Public TV's total national, regional and local revenue in FY00 totaled $1.6 billion, according to the Corporation for Public Broadcasting (CPB). Leading sources of revenue: members (23.5%); state governments (18.3%); CPB and federal grants/contracts (16.4%); businesses (16.1%); state colleges and universities (6.5%); and foundations (5.5%).

    Note that the total of government sources in this list is 41.2% of total revenue, which equates to around 650 million dollars. That's very similar to Commonwealth funds to Australia's ABC. Of course, the US taxpayer base supporting that level of public subsidy is much larger, but Australia's geographical size is as great, and it's going to cost as much to deliver a localised service to the whole continent (which both ABC and PBS do) irrespective of population. Moreover, ABC does lots of things (AM and FM radio, orchestras) that PBS doesn't.

    The neoliberals' main argument seems to be that people wanting to view "quality" programming can do so on cable TV. Moreover, perusal of cable programming, both here and in the US, simply doesn't support the proposition that it offers the sort of diversity and quality provided by ABC and SBS. Bruce Springsteen's "Fifty Seven Channels and Nothing On" isn't just an empty song lyric.

    The PBS website boasts that some 70.9% of American TV-owning families watch a PBS station every month, for an average of 7.5 hours. No doubt there are figures available for the ABC, and I'd bet they'd be significantly higher. ABC and SBS don't usually figure in the ratings, but the vast majority of Australians regularly enrich their lives by watching the programs they screen. Our lives would be much poorer if they weren't there.

    It seems to me that ul;timately this sort of discussion comes down to whether your underlying political philosophy is classical liberal, neoliberal (which I would define as economic/market liberalism shorn of all social elements), social democratic, socialist etc, or somewhere in between those broad general boxes, or whether (like me) you're a pragmatist prepared to dip into any of these boxes to find a combination of approaches that produces the Good Society, which I define as one which values both individual liberty and equality of opportunity, as well as diversity (which contains elements of both), and which cares for our fellow members of society by providing a strong social security safety net for those unable to take care of themselves and encouraging/socialising (but not coercing) individuals to act with justice and benevolence.

    Applying those principles (and my pragmatic intuitions) to the issue of whether particular enterprises or institutions should be publicly owned, I conclude that public ownership is appropriate where:

    (1) an area involves natural monopoly;
    (2) private enterprise doesn't provide sufficient diversity or excellence;
    (3) the area assists substantially in promoting equality of opportunity.

    It seems to me that the continued existence of ABC and SBS is justified by both (2) and (3) above, but those questions come down to whether private enterprise IS able to provide sufficient diversity and excellence in a way that promotes equality of opportunity. Yobbo seems to claim that cable TV provides everything that ABC and SBS do, whereas most of the rest of us seem to disagree (I know I do, and you only need to look at the weekly TV guide to conclude that I'm right).

    However, Yobbo's argument might still have merit, because we don't know what would happen if the ABC and SBS didn't exist. It's conceivable that similar cable channels don't currently exist because the existence of ABC and SBS as free-to-air channels makes it commercially non-viable to create them. Personally, I don't think it's worth destroying invaluable public resources like ABC and SBS merely to find out the answer. Moreover, even if cable equivalents DID spring up, that wouldn't fully address the equality of opportunity justification. Access to basic cable isn't cheap for many aged, unemployed and otherwise disadvantaged people, and one suspects that private versions of ABC and SBS would probably attract a substantial premium service loading (i.e. not be bundled with the basic service, because it would be very expensive to create such services by comparison with just grabbing existing available programming - which is almost entirely what the basic cable channels do). Yobbo seems to assume conclusively that the audience for ABC and SBS consists of wealthy members of the "chattering classes" who can (and should) well afford to pay for their pleasures. But I suspect that assumption is wrong (or at least partly so). I don't have time right now to look for any survey material showing the demographic breakup of ABC and SBS audiences. Maybe others might like to explore this angle.

    Gary,

    The only essential things are food, water, shelter and sex (which with any luck we don't need to purchase). But most of us think there's more to a fulfilling life than that, and more than will be provided by a purely private market-based model which results in not much more than the lowest common denominator mass taste being satisfied (because, although I reckon most people want more than just big sport, reality TV, quizzes and cop shows, there isn't a single audience big enough to sustain any wider choices than that without public subsidy).

    The "bottom line" of all this is that this is a fairly pointless (if enoyable) debate. No-one except a few neoliberal devotees in the blogosphere, media and a couple of right wing thinktanks even wants the ABC and SBS to be abolished or privatised. Politicians aren't going to touch it with a barge pole, because they know it would be political suicide. The Tories love whingeing about ABC bias, but that's mostly self-indulgent playing to their gallery of core supporters.

    Apart from electoral suicide, it's a safe bet that most politicians and senior bureaucrats on both sides are avid ABC viewers too, so they're not going to cut off their noses to spite their ideological faces.

    Lastly, even Rupert Murdoch doesn't want to abolish the ABC. The reason why cable TV is even marginally viable in a small, dispersed market like Australia is that almost all Foxtel's programming is rehashed stuff made by someone else (including the ABC). If they had to make or fund their own programming to any significant extent, instead of buying cheap existing stuff, they'd go broke very rapidly. So Rupert's interests are best served by keeping all the free-to-airs in place (including ABC and SBS) and producing programming that he can later buy cheaply, but making sure there aren't too many of them and that they don't have enough money to be too good. That way, it preserves a market for the wider range (than free-to-air) of re-runs and sport that Foxtel mostly survives on.
