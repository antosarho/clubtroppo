Elect the G-G

Posted on January 30, 2008 by Ken Parish
Posted in Politics - national, Law

Seamus C's post proposing popular elections for Australian of the Year raises the intriguing possibility of a similar mechanism for appointment of a rather more important official Australian role, namely that of Governor-General. There was speculation only a week or so ago that the Rudd government might appoint former Labor leader Kim Beazley as the next G-G, but it was quickly scotched by Rudd himself:

"The next Governor-General of Australia will not be a former or serving politician - conservative or Labor," he said. "The reason being is, I believe it's an office which is often best discharged by someone from the broader community."

Few will disagree, but such an approach does nothing to prepare the ground for a future Australian republic, which is a relevant issue for those of us who still think it's a goal worth pursuing.

The current consensus seems to be that there's little point broaching the republican issue again until the current Queen dies and is replaced by King Charles and Queen Camilla.  What that view ignores is that, even in that situation, the republican cause will get precisely nowhere until we achieve at least an informal resolution of the dilemma about how to appoint and dismiss a President to replace the present constitutional combination of Queen and Governor-General, especially in relation to the key constitutional function of the President/G-G's function of dismissing an elected government.  Should a President be popularly elected, or appointed by (or on the recommendation of) the Prime Minister or government of the day as at present?

The problem is that opinion polling tends to indicate that a strong majority of the Australian public favours a directly elected President, while by contrast many political actors on both sides fear that an elected President would receive a popular mandate that might result in his/her developing a real (rather than just symbolic) executive role, which could destabilise our current tried-and-true Westminster-based system.  A President with a popular national mandate might embarrass the government of the day by speaking out on controversial issues (as Sir William Deane did even without a popular mandate) or even be tempted to breach convention in a more serious way e.g. by refusing or threatening to refuse to sign controversial bills into law.

These sorts of fears are exaggerated. Whether a President could effectively hold an executive government to ransom in a substantive way would depend largely on the applicable dismissal mechanism.  If the PM can dismiss the president (as was the case with the 1999 referendum model) or, preferably, if we duplicate as closely as possible the current system (where the PM merely advises the Queen to dismiss and she must ultimately but not necessarily instantly do so in accordance with convention), then there can be no problem.  A President who exceeded his/her constitutional role would be sacked in fairly short order and the elected government would prevail.

As for the possibility that an elected President might feel greater freedom than an appointed one to speak out on controversial issues, no doubt it's a peril some politicians would prefer to avoid but it's hardly a compelling argument in a free liberal democracy like Australia where the executive government already arguably has too much power. If the Rudd government had any interest at all in moving incrementally towards a republic, it would immediately introduce a new system for appointment of the Governor-General whereby all parties in Federal Parliament meet and agree on a short list of (say) 3 candidates for Governor-General, with a national popular election then being held to settle on a final recommendation for appointment.  I don't think any new legislation would even be needed to implement such a system; in a constitutional sense the appointment would still be by the Queen on the advice of the Prime Minister, but it would be extraordinary for the PM to do anything but recommend the candidate who had received the most votes at the election.

By adopting such a system informally now, it will have become the status quo by the time the republic debate rears its head again after QEII dies.  Most skeptics will have accepted that the sky hasn't fallen and isn't likely to do so, the population at large will be much less susceptible to the sort of unprincipled and dishonest fear campaign the monarchists ran last time, and we will have had a chance to fine-tune the system before putting it to referendum and entrenching it in the Constitution.  It's time!  Elect the G-G!

    A few comments on the comments so far:

    "Is it the mandate problem - as some suggest? or the idiots might get elected problem - as others suggest? Both?"

    With the system I suggested above, the "idiots might get elected" problem should not exist. I was suggesting a system where Parliament (either by consensus or by - say - an 80% majority) preselects a slate of suitable candidates. Alan Jones, Maria Venuti etc certainly wouldn't make the cut, although I wouldn't entirely rule out Eddie McGuire (and in fact he wouldn't make a bad G-G - though probably a bit young).

    It's the mandate problem that seems to concern politicians. However, as I said, it's overstated. It's certainly more than likely that an elected G-G would feel entitled to comment publicly as a matter of conscience on some important and controversial issues, just as Bill Deane did on a couple of indigenous issues. I don't see why politicians need to be overly concerned about such a development, although in practice it no doubt explains why this solution isn't being seriously canvassed. Such a role would serve in part to rebuild a democratic check and balance (albeit a modest one) on the executive government, where those checks have been eroded by the evolution of the Australian political system to favour a very rigid system of party discipline whereby the executive government and especially the PM have acquired excessive power by comparison with other Westminster-derived systems.

    On Geoff Honnor's point that we need first to work out what we want the G-G to do, most of the role is already spelled out by the combination of the text of the Constitution and conventions. He/she mostly does what they're told by the elected government, but has crucial reserve powers to act on his own initiative in relation to only a few of his powers and in a narrow range of circumstances. It's been a while since I read David Solomon's book, but I certainly don't accept that the mere fact of the G-G being elected results in all or even most of the existing conventions being redundant or in the G-G being free to exercise constitutional power in his own right to any greater extent than now. Any attempt to do so would result in the PM advising the Queen to sack him and the Queen accepting that advice without hesitation.

    Similarly, and still on Geoff's question of what we want the G-G to do, it has always (until recently) been the case that the G-G has a strong ceremonial role, farewelling troops, opening bridges and public festivals etc. John Howard largely hijacked that role for himself, presumably partly out of personal ego/hubris and partly for tactical political reasons to appropriate a quasi-presidential "l'etat c'est moi" aura to himself. One would hope that this function now reverts to the G-G where it belongs.

    Penultimately, on Nicholas Gruen's response to the effect that he sees the whole thing as rather a third order issue and even something of a Monty Pythonesque joke which he is happy to see continue indefinitely, the problem with that attitude is that on rare but crucially important occasions the G-G fufils a central role in our constitutional system. Those occasions have only occurred twice in the last hundred years (the Whitlam and Lang dismissals - the latter being a State level matter, but the principles are the same), but that's quite enough for us to treat the issue seriously IMO.

    Moreover, the events surrounding the Whitlam dismissal indicate that the current system should now be regarded as broken and in need of repair. Our system at its inception was supposed to afford the Queen a crucial "circuit breaker" role in relation to a Whitlam-type dismissal situation. That is, one would have expected the G-G to consult and seek advice from the Queen before embarking on a process leading to Whitlam's dismissal. Had he kept Her Maj informed and obtained her advice first, he no doubt would not have needed to fear that Whitlam might gazump him and pre-emptively get the Queen to sack him (Kerr) before he had been able to discharge his own constitutional function. However, and in part because even back in 1975 the Queen was already perceived by most Australians as entirely extraneous to our domestic political affairs, Kerr chose not to involve the Queen at all and therefore convinced himself that the only way to avoid the danger of Whitlam pre-emptively getting the Queen to sack him was to ambush Whitlam and sack him first without prior warning. It was this more than anything else that was the problematic and utterly undemocratic aspect of the entire situation.

    Moreover, subsequent events mean that it is now even more unthinkable that a G-G would involve the Queen in an Australian political/constitutional crisis. Hence the Queen's crucial circuit breaker role is effectively moribund. That is potentially extremely serious if/when another constitutional crisis occurs (as eventually one must). Now, merely making the office of G-G an elected one won't cure that defect, but it's a first step towards a republican model that SHOULD IMO have as a key objective the restoration of a modern and completely localised version of the Queen's old "circuit breaker" role. Until we break through the seemingly insoluble stand-off between those favouring respectively elective and appointed models for the HoS, we cannot proceed to the next step of amending the Constitution to restore a viable mechanism for the interplay between the dismissal of an elected government, the role of the HoS and effective mechanisms to ensure the accountability of the latter.

    Answering one of James Farrell's questions at this point, I don't see any way we will ever be able to move to a republic except by an elected G-G/Presidency/HoS model. The strong and persistent public sentiment in favour of the elective model might well be partially a result of ignorance (as several commenters argue), but even if it is I don't see that situation changing. Voters were deluged with information before and after the 1999 referendum about the role of the G-G, but it didn't shake their strong view that they wanted to elect the HoS. I don't see that changing in the future. Nor do I necessarily accept that wanting to elect the HoS is an ignorant stance. If the HoS (whether called G-G or President) is indeed our formal ceremonial Head of State who represents the nation on important international occasions; if he/she also has the role of speaking out judiciously and as a matter of conscience on some controversial issues of national significance; and if he/she has a real and vital constitutional role (the reserve powers situation) albeit one exercised only infrequently; then it hardly seems unreasonable that the people of Australia rather than just the politicians should select the holder of that important public office.

    My own view is that the Constitution should eventually be amended (on moving to a republican model) so that the G-G/President (I actually agree with Jacques' suggestion that we should continue to call the office G-G rather than President) should be popularly elected from a short list selected by a parliamentary supermajority; that the G-G's formal constitutional functions should remain in all respects unaltered; and that the G-G himself/herself should be dismissable on advice of the PM to the most senior serving State Governor (who would then fulfil the "circuit breaker" role traditionally filled by the Queen i.e. he would ultimately be obliged by convention to accept the PM's advice to sack the G-G, but could in appropriate circumstances take his time in doing so and insist on being fully informed and advising and warning the PM before doing so - thereby giving a G-G in a Whitlam-type situation the scope to issue an ultimatum giving the PM a reasonable time to remedy the situation giving rise to the threat of dismissal of the government).

    In the meantime, however, we should give a trial run to a system involving electing the G-G under the current unamended Constitution. If we don't start making cautious, incremental moves in a republican direction in the near future, we run a severe risk that the advent of King Charles and Queen Camilla, and their turning the monarchy into an even more caricatured soap opera joke than it already is, will provoke an ill-considered populist stampede towards a defective republican model.

    Mike

    I agree with your proposal that sovereignty should be expressed to reside with "the people" (several High Court Justices reckon it already does, albeit by implication), and I also think your proposal for a "one horse race" popular election/approval process for G-G is a good one which addresses most of the cogent objections to an election model.

    My problem, as I thought I'd made clear but now suspect I didn't, is that a similar election/plebiscite for dismissal of the G-G would be dangerous and ill-conceived. The system's stability would be dependent on always selecting a G-G prepared to exercise restraint and observe constitutional conventions, because if he/she didn't they might well prove effectively unsackable. You argue that a G-G has never been dismissed and therefore it's an unimportant issue. But you ignore the strong possibility that it hasn't happened because the current system ensures that it doesn't.

    A G-G who (for example) tried to block legislation with which he strongly disagreed (a far more plausible situation than the Whitlam dismissal one) would immediately be sacked by the Queen on the advice of the PM under the current system, and the blocked legislation would be immediately signed by his appointed successor. I strongly suspect, however, that numerous G-Gs have in fact privately disagreed strongly enough with particular pieces of government legislation that they would have at least been tempted to block them if they had had an effective ability to do so. Under your suggested system they would have that ability. For example, an elected G-G who disagreed with (say) Howard's unpopular GST legislation a few years ago could have blocked it and may well then have proved practically unsackable. Just as it's very easy to whip up enough opposition to even a perfectly sensible referendum to ensure a "no" vote, so it would be easy to ensure that a majority voted against the sacking of a popularly elected G-G who had blocked an unpopular piece of legislation. That would be a radical change in our system of government, which isn't to say that it shouldn't be considered, but one shouldn't present it as a minimalist change when in reality it is anything but.

    My own view is that your proposal for express popular sovereignty and popular nomination and approval of the G-G, along with my proposal for dismissal of an errant G-G by the most senior serving State Governor advised by the PM and acting in accordance with the present and longstanding conventions about their behaviour in such situations, would deliver the best of all worlds in this regard.
